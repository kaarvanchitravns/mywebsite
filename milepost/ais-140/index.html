AIS-140 explained: which vehicles need a VLTD, and what it must do

A plain reading of the rule, for owners who have been told they need a tracker and want to know why.

6 minute read · last checked 20 November 2026

What AIS-140 actually is

AIS-140 is not a product and not a brand. It is a technical standard written by the Automotive Research Association of India and notified by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988. It sets out the minimum a Vehicle Location Tracking Device — a VLTD — has to do when it is fitted to a commercial vehicle.1

The standard was brought in through MoRTH notification GSR 979(E), dated 28 September 2018.2 When a dealer tells you a tracker is “AIS-140 certified”, he is saying the model has been type-approved against that standard — not that it is a better tracker.

Which vehicles are covered

Rule 125H of the Central Motor Vehicles Rules made a VLTD with emergency buttons mandatory for new public service vehicles registered from 1 January 2019, and left each state to notify its own dates for vehicles already on the road.3 Beyond public service vehicles, the central rules reach national permit goods carriers, and N2 and N3 vehicles carrying hazardous goods.3

This is the part that causes most of the confusion in a transport office. Enforcement sits with the states, not with Delhi. The dates, the empanelment list and the degree of strictness differ from one state to the next, so the only answer that is reliable for your vehicle is the one your own RTO gives.4

What changed in 2026

In May 2026 the Supreme Court directed that no public service vehicle be granted a fitness certificate or a permit unless its VLTD and emergency buttons have been verified and recorded on VAHAN — after noting that fewer than one per cent of transport vehicles actually had a working VLTD.3

For an owner, that is the whole significance of the subject. A tracker has stopped being something an RTO may or may not ask about, and has become a condition of the paperwork that keeps the vehicle earning.

What the device has to do

  • Report position continuously to a state-approved backend, not only to a private app.5
  • Carry one or more emergency buttons a driver or passenger can press to raise an alert.5
  • Support NavIC alongside GPS. India’s own satellite system is part of the standard, which is one reason an ordinary tracker cannot be made compliant by relabelling it.2
  • Be type-approved by a notified testing agency — in practice ARAI or ICAT — and appear on the approved list of the state where the vehicle is registered.4

School buses and vans are treated more strictly again: a VLTD with a panic button is required across the country, and several states — Delhi, Haryana and Maharashtra among them — additionally require camera surveillance.6

Before you buy

Three questions settle most of it. Is this exact model on my state’s approved list? Not the brand — the model. Who does the backend reporting to the state portal, and for how long is it included? A device that stops reporting is a device that has stopped complying. What happens at renewal? The SIM, the subscription and the certificate all have dates on them, and a vehicle fails its fitness check on whichever expires first.

If the rule does not cover your vehicle

Plenty of goods vehicles fall outside the mandate. They still get lost, idle, take detours and run up fuel. For those, an ordinary tracker does the job at a lower price, and there is no reason to pay for certification you are not required to hold. The mistake is the other way round — buying a plain tracker for a vehicle the rule does cover, and discovering it at the fitness test.

Common questions

Is AIS-140 mandatory?

Yes, for every category the central rules cover: public service vehicles registered from 1 January 2019, national permit goods carriers, and N2 and N3 vehicles carrying hazardous goods. States notify their own dates for older vehicles and enforce the rule themselves, so check with the RTO where the vehicle is registered.

What is the difference between a VLTD and an ordinary GPS tracker?

A VLTD is type-approved against AIS-140, supports NavIC as well as GPS, carries emergency buttons and reports to a state-approved backend. An ordinary tracker reports only to a private app and cannot be made compliant by relabelling.

What happens if my vehicle does not have one?

Since a May 2026 Supreme Court direction, a public service vehicle cannot be granted a fitness certificate or permit unless its VLTD and emergency buttons are verified on VAHAN.
