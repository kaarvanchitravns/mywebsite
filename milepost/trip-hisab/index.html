How to keep a trip hisab that actually balances

Not a lecture on bookkeeping. The habits that decide whether you know what a trip earned.

5 minute read · last checked 20 November 2026

Where the money actually goes missing

Ask most owners what a trip earned and you get a number that is roughly right. Roughly right is the problem. The freight is known to the rupee, and so is the diesel if the pump slip survived. Everything else — the ten rupees at a check post, the mechanic on the highway, the advance given at 2am over the phone, the loading charge settled in cash — arrives late, in pieces, and attaches itself to no particular trip.

By the time the vehicle is home, the trip is over and the expenses are still arriving. They end up in a single pile for the month, and the month tells you whether you made money. It does not tell you which route, which party or which driver made it.

The one rule that fixes most of it

Open the trip before the vehicle moves, and attach every rupee to it as it is spent. Not at the end of the week. Not from memory. The moment money leaves, it goes against that trip number.

This sounds obvious and it is, but it is also the single difference between accounts that balance and accounts that nearly balance. A trip that exists before the journey has somewhere to put the expense when the driver calls from the road. A trip created afterwards is a reconstruction, and reconstructions are generous to whoever is doing the reconstructing.

What a trip record needs

  • Vehicle and driver. Both, by name. A trip belongs to a vehicle; the hisab belongs to a driver.
  • Party, route, and the freight agreed — recorded when it is agreed, not when it is paid.
  • Advances given, each with a date. This is the line that causes the most arguments at settlement.
  • Diesel, tolls, loading, unloading, repairs, check posts, driver’s expenses. Each against the trip.
  • Kilometres run. Without it you have costs but no cost per kilometre, which is the only number that compares one route to another.
  • What the party still owes. A trip is not finished when the vehicle returns. It is finished when the money arrives.

Settling with a driver

Most driver disputes are not about honesty. They are about two different memories of the same month. One man remembers an advance in cash at a dhaba; the other remembers it as part of the previous trip. Neither is lying, and neither can prove it.

A running khata per driver — advances on one side, expenses he has accounted for on the other, balance at the bottom — removes the argument entirely, because both of you are reading the same page. Settle at the end of each trip rather than the end of each month, while both of you still remember.

What you get for the discipline

Three numbers you probably do not have today. What a route actually earns, after everything, so you can decline the ones that do not. What a vehicle costs per kilometre, which tells you when a truck has stopped being worth its repairs. And what is outstanding, from whom, and for how long — which is usually the largest sum in the business and the least precisely known.

None of this requires software. A disciplined notebook will do it, and plenty of good operators run on one. What software changes is who does the typing: when the tolls arrive from the tag and the kilometres from the tracker, the only things left to enter by hand are the ones a human actually witnessed.
